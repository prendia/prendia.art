# The Livelong Day - Companion Piece

Below are some collated works on the topic.

## In Sound
There is an album by the Irish group Lankum by the same name (link). It sounds like time made heavy. It’s old and slow and full of mixed weather. They touch on themes that ask the listener to be present in both today’s society and in stories of those who came before.

Each song is carried by its own repetitive melody. Each melody builds throughout the length of the song, sometimes arriving at something brooding, other times something unexpectedly hopeful. The repetition doesn't resolve so much as deepen.

That quality (the repetitive sound building into atmosphere) feels aligned with those days that stretch and won’t end. Music that doesn’t ask you to move through it, but to sit beside it.

There's a word in music for this: drone. A sustained note or chord held beneath everything else. Many of Lankum's songs are built on it. The drone doesn't progress, it continues. Somehow, within the continuation, feeling accumulates. It’s not unlike a livelong day itself.

Perhaps there is a quiet gift hidden inside the Livelong Day. Being forced to sit inside time with nothing but feeling.

## In Written Word
The moral weight of how we spend our days sits heavy on some people. Perhaps this is part of what makes certain days drag. Not just idleness, but the awareness of idleness. The feeling of a day not yet deserved.

There is a short poem by George Eliot called Count That Day Lost (link). She writes that the days in which at least one act of kindness is taken, ‘one self-denying deed’, are the days well spent. But on the livelong day, in which ‘you've cheered no heart, nor helped a struggling soul’, that day, she says, count as lost.

It's a quietly devastating idea. The day didn't drag. It was forfeit.

The Russian writer Tolstoy returned to this question again and again. In The Death of Ivan Ilyich, his protagonist spends his final days trapped in a slowing, agonising stretch of time. They’re unable to move forward, unable to return. Every hour becomes enormous. The livelong day is, in that story, the entire shape of a wasted life made visible only at its end.

## A Note
There is a version of the livelong day that seems not to be suffering. Perhaps it’s the long summer afternoon of childhood. Or the slow Sunday that has nowhere to be.

In these moments, the very same fullness of time becomes something else entirely. Not drag, but depth.

I wonder the difference here. Perhaps it is simply the skill of surrendering to it.
