# The Livelong Day

Some days are livelong days.

Monotonous days. Where the passing of time feels full and whole and every minute feels like a lifetime. Time doesn’t pass so much as just accumulate with a slow drag felt in your body. You want nothing more than for it to move, but the wanting only seems to slow it further.

This feeling has been explored for centuries across sound, poetry and the visual arts. There is a companion thought here that explores some of these.

## The original meaning

The phrase ‘livelong day’ has shifted meaning considerably and I do wonder if this shift tells us something.

The word has Germanic roots, with traces in Old English as far back as the 15th century. At its origin, it had nothing to do with endurance. It meant something closer to the opposite.

Old English ~ lief / loef: dear, beloved (c.1250)
Middle English ~ leve-long, lefe-long
Contemporary English idiom ~ all the livelong day

Old German idiom ~ den lieben langen Tag: the dear long day (often used to describe children playing all day)
Contemporary German idiom ~ den lieben langen Tag (still in use)

Every one of these early forms is oriented toward something positive. A dearly beloved day. A full day in the warmest sense.


## From a beloved bay to an exhaustive day

It is supposed that the meaning shifted during the 19th and 20th centuries with the rise of the Industrial Revolution.

The livelong day became the working day. Repetitive labour. Exhaustion. Drudgery. What was once beloved became something to be endured.

I find myself wondering whether this is simply a linguistic curiosity, or whether it's something more like a record. Perhaps a trace of how people’s relationship with time changed.

The industrial clock didn't just structure the day; it seems to have recoloured the language around it.

And so, in a time where the day was not treasured but took perseverance to finish, working music emerged.

## Work songs

I’ve been working on the railroad all the livelong day. Miner's songs. The sea shanty. The songs were not just expressions of labour, but perhaps technologies of endurance. The possibility of using rhythm to carve time into bearable pieces, to make hours that no longer belonged to them feel, at least in part, survivable. The phrase embedded itself further into the culture, and further from its origin.

## What we’ve lost

English once had a word for something different.

The lief-long day. The dear, long, beloved day. A day that felt full not because it was slow, but because it was loved.

We don't seem to have that anymore. What we have instead is the livelong day, heavy, something to get through.

I've been trying to think of a word that holds both qualities at once: long and beloved. The closest I can find may be halcyon? Though it leans more toward peace and nostalgia than duration. Or perhaps a blessed day, though that carries its own weight.

Perhaps it's the absence of such a word that is the point.
